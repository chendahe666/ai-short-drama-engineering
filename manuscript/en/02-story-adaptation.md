# Volume 2: Story Design and Adaptation

AI Short-Drama Production Engineering · Trial edition v0.1 · 2026-10-13

This volume aims to turn a genre or premise into an episodic script that viewers can understand, in which characters take action and the ending delivers a payoff. The following creative methods are recommendations synthesized for this project. Research findings are accompanied by their sources and limitations.

## Chapter 1: Identify What Viewers Hope to Get

“A satisfying workplace drama” is only a category; it cannot generate a story on its own. A more useful formulation is that viewers see a person being asked to take the blame for an error regain the ability to choose through actions that carry a cost. This explains why the character deserves attention and defines what counts as payoff at the end. Simply adding a boss shouting, coworkers mocking, and luxury cars arriving does not necessarily advance that promise.

Write the idea in four sentences: who is in what situation; what they want to do now; who or what stands in their way; and what, specifically, they will lose if they fail. In *Back for Revision*, Lu Qiao receives printed samples bearing the wrong date. She wants to check the approved version before signing a responsibility form. Her supervisor pressures her as the delivery deadline approaches. She risks being held responsible for rework and losing the client's trust.

The stakes should be visible in the scene: a responsibility form pushed toward someone, an approaching delivery commitment, or a question from the client is more concrete than narration saying “she was under enormous pressure.” These details do not all have to be explained at once in the opening.

In actual audience feedback, some viewers enjoy reversals and romance, while others complain about repeated humiliation and characters who do not respond to evidence. Such comments point us toward studying whether expectations are fulfilled; they do not establish that everyone wants the same kind of satisfaction. [GM11: Discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/ReelShorts/comments/1vgcx4s/the_ai_movies/)

Exercise: Rewrite “put the boss in his place” using the four sentences above. Answer guide: include a specific goal, obstacle, and loss. If the only action remains “wait for someone to discover how capable she is,” you do not yet have an actionable scene.

## Chapter 2: Characters Drive Causality Through Choices

We use a simple action table: what a character knows, what they want, what choices they have, and what each choice costs. This is not a psychological law. It is a working tool for preventing the plot from relying on evidence that the writer supplies conveniently at the last minute.

Lu Qiao knows she submitted two versions but is unsure which one the printer received. She can sign, argue, or ask to review the records together. Signing is fastest but costs her the chance to explain. Arguing expresses her frustration but may delay delivery. A joint review requires the supervisor and client to participate and may expose management problems within the team. Choices with costs give a character agency.

Han Zheng cannot lose all common sense merely because he is the antagonist. He might initially trust the proof on the table, question whether the version was sent after seeing the chat record, and then admit that he bypassed approval when confronted with the client's confirmation. Each change in the evidence should change his judgment or strategy. A character can be evasive, but the evasion needs a new approach rather than the same denial repeated.

Link the story as “because A, B happens; B forces a character to choose C; C brings about D.” For example, the client changes the date, so Lu Qiao uploads v8. The supervisor sends v7 to print without waiting for client confirmation, causing the error. Pressure to accept responsibility prompts Lu Qiao to request a three-party review. The three participants establish the sequence of the date change and the print submission, correcting the judgment about responsibility. Freeze this causal version first; the subsequent script, dates on props, and shots must all use the same facts.

Counterexample: a chairperson who has never been mentioned appears at the end and immediately promotes Lu Qiao. This may provide brief satisfaction, but it does not fulfill the promise that she will regain agency through her own actions. If an identity reveal is intentional, that identity needs to become a traceable clue earlier in the story.

Exercise: Remove one instance in which chance rescues a character in your script and replace it with an earlier action by the protagonist. Check that the action was reasonable at the time, rather than existing only to plant a tool for the ending.

## Chapter 3: Beats and Episodes Operate at Different Levels

A beat is a change: Lu Qiao notices the error, decides to pause before signing, discovers a version conflict, and gains an opportunity for a joint review. These four changes can be distributed across different shots. Cutting to ten close-ups does not create ten story beats if the characters' situation has not changed.

Check each scene using “entry state → action → resistance → exit state.” At the start of Episode 2, Lu Qiao has already discovered that a different version was sent to print but still needs to check the review requirement following the date change. By the end, all three participants have reviewed the records, and the client has explicitly stated that the new draft was not yet approved. Even before the remedy is complete, she should make some progress so viewers do not feel the episode accomplished nothing.

An episode ending can create a question, change a relationship, make a decision, or complete a local victory. Suspense is not limited to “who is on the other end of the phone?” In this case, the reason involving the old link has emerged. The client arrives and discovers that samples have already been printed before the new draft was confirmed. The next question becomes how to repair the damage and take responsibility for action.

Existing experiments do not establish that cliffhangers always win. Wirz and colleagues did not detect an increase in the intention to continue watching from cliffhanger endings in a long-form drama experiment. Schibler and colleagues found an increase in the desire for the next installment in some comparisons in written-narrative experiments. The materials, durations, controls, and outcomes differ, so the results cannot simply be combined into a rule for short dramas. [FM09](https://sonar.ch/unifr/documents/319990); [FM10](https://doi.org/10.1080/15213269.2023.2219456)

The testable recommendation adopted in this project is that each episode should contain at least one identifiable, meaningful change. When another episode is needed, leave a specific question arising from that change. This is not a requirement for every episode to include an argument, a reversal, or a cut to black.

Exercise: Write two different endings. Version A cuts off before the client speaks. Version B lets the client first confirm receipt of v8, then ask who approved sending the file to print. When testing, compare comprehension and the desire to continue, rather than asking only which version is more exciting.

## Chapter 4: The Opening Should Establish Meaning Quickly

A “fast opening” should mean that viewers learn early why something is worth their attention; it does not necessarily mean adding more information every second. In this case, the opening could show the wrong date and responsibility form before clarifying the relationships. Alternatively, it could begin with the client questioning the protagonist, then cut back ten minutes. The latter increases the burden of understanding time and is worth using only if the benefit is sufficient.

TikTok's guidance for performance advertising emphasizes an early proposition and hook, while treating those suggestions as starting points for further testing. Ad recall and story comprehension are different goals, so we do not turn advertising timings directly into a rigid clock for dramatic scenes. [FM07](https://ads.tiktok.com/resources/help/article/creative-best-practices?lang=en)

A practical review of an opening asks whether viewers can say who is in what trouble after the first short section, what they want to know next, and whether they have misunderstood the type of work or the relationships between characters. If they can repeat only “someone was shouting,” the sound may be loud, but the story has not yet been established.

Dialogue does more than transmit information; it also expresses relationships. Han Zheng saying “Sign this first” and saying “We'll put this one under your name for now; I'll explain it to the client” reveal different strategies. Do not make characters mechanically repeat known facts three times for the audience. Necessary repetition should change the tone, evidence, or stakes.

Exercise: Write three openings for the same main content: one built around an object that serves as evidence, one around a character's choice, and one that cold-opens on an outcome. There is no predetermined winner. Assess whether each opening promises an experience that the main story will actually deliver.

## Chapter 5: Keep an Explainable Mapping in Adaptation

Once you have the source work, distinguish possession of the text, permission to read it, and adaptation rights. For a commercial project, the scope to verify includes format, language, territory, term, exclusivity, and uses such as trailers. A platform entry point does not replace a specific authorization.

For each source passage, record whether it is “retained, condensed, merged, moved earlier, added, or removed,” along with the reason and any changed dependencies. Moving evidence from the ending to an earlier point might increase suspense in the opening but could also let a character know the truth too early. You must check whether their later behavior still makes sense. Merging characters may also create a situation in which one person would have to be in two places at once.

First turn internal thought into visible choice, then consider voice-over. “She knew that putting up with this any longer would ruin her” could become reaching for a pen, stopping, turning the responsibility form over, and asking who approved it. Voice-over can still compress time and space, introduce subjective irony, or add a perspective. We do not require all interiority to be removed.

Existing short-drama skills maintain these connections through source analysis, storyboarding, and review. They can serve as implementation tools, but their rules still need to be adapted to the project. [TS01](https://github.com/zenstory-ai/drama-skills)

*Back for Revision* is an independent exercise created for this textbook. Authorization for the previously selected novel still needs to be verified. We do not claim to have obtained that author's permission, and we have not presented the teaching case as a completed screen adaptation of the novel.

Exercise: Choose a story of 300 Chinese characters for which you hold the necessary rights, and make a six-row adaptation mapping table. Answer guide: each removal or change should identify its effect on character knowledge, the sequence of events, or what must be delivered visually.

## Chapter 6: Bring the Script into an Iteration Process

AI is useful for proposing alternative structures, finding contradictions, and organizing character knowledge and versions. Its self-assigned score of “9” does not represent audience experience. Script review should first address problems that can be identified clearly: evidence appears after it is used, a character knows something without a reason, or a misunderstanding resolved in the previous episode is reset without justification.

We recommend three layers of revision: first correct facts and causality; next adjust when information reaches the audience; then refine dialogue, emotion, and pacing. If the facts of the client's date change have not been settled, changing the font or switching models will not solve the story problem.

A creator-interview study describes how comments participate in the creative process, but the researchers explicitly state a limitation: the material comes from interviews, not direct audience behavior or platform causal data. Comments therefore provide leads about problems; whether a revision works requires fresh observation. [FM11: Methods, Section 3, and Limitations, Section 5.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14045v1)

We recommend keeping seven columns: “before revision, triggering problem, source, after revision, affected episodes, validation method, and result.” If someone says “I cannot tell which date is right,” first check whether props, dialogue, and subtitles agree. If subtitles obscure the evidence, changing the story could damage the causal chain instead of fixing the problem.

Final exercise: Submit a three-episode script and a causality review. Acceptance does not mean predicting a hit. It means the goal can be restated, each episode contains a change, key evidence has a source, the ending fulfills the promise, and unresolved questions have been left deliberately. Human viewing and market performance still require separate tests.
